\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}

We first transfer the total-depth gaps to trivially perfect
completion. The YES deletion sets and NO separator bounds also
give hardness for deletion and labeling objectives. Established
automata and hypergraph reductions transfer the parameter gaps,
while the linear treewidth gaps yield exponential lower bounds
for junction-tree table size.

\subsection{Trivially perfect completion by number of added edges}

The separator bound controls total elimination depth, which in turn
determines the number of edges in a trivially perfect completion.
An approximation transfer must also account for the edges already
present in the input.

The \emph{closure} of a rooted forest joins each vertex to all its
proper ancestors. A graph is \emph{trivially perfect} if and only if
it is such a closure. Let $f(J)$ be the minimum number of edges
added to $J$ to obtain a trivially perfect graph, and recall that
$\totd(J)$ is its minimum total elimination depth.
The completion identity~\cite[Theorem~8]{HogemoEtAl2021} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tp-fill-depth}
 f(J)=\totd(J)-|V(J)|-|E(J)|.
\end{equation}
Indeed, a forest closure has one edge from each vertex to each
proper ancestor, so its number of edges is
$\sum_v(\operatorname{depth}_F(v)-1)$. Every trivially perfect
graph is a forest closure. Requiring it to contain $J$ is equivalent
to the elimination-forest condition. Subtracting the original edges
gives the identity.

For any $C\geq1$, a factor-$C$ completion of cost $z$ satisfies
$f(J)\leq z\leq C f(J)$. Adding the graph order and the number
of original edges gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tp-adjusted-cost}
\begin{aligned}
 \totd(J)
 &=f(J)+|V(J)|+|E(J)|\\
 &\leq z+|V(J)|+|E(J)|
 \leq C f(J)+|V(J)|+|E(J)|\\
 &\leq C\bigl(f(J)+|V(J)|+|E(J)|\bigr)
 =C\totd(J),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the final inequality uses $C\ge1$.
Thus Corollary~\ref{cor:total-depth-bounded-degree} also gives NP-hardness
within some absolute factor $1+\epsilon_0>1$ and UGC hardness
below $8/7$ for trivially perfect completion on graphs of bounded
maximum degree. A supplied trivially perfect supergraph has a
forest representation, whose total depth equals its number of edges
plus its order, so the adjusted cost also supplies the
forest needed by the NP extraction argument.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tp-completion-sse}
Every fixed constant-factor approximation for the minimum number
of edges added to obtain a trivially perfect graph is SSE-hard,
including on graphs of bounded maximum degree.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply~\eqref{eq:tp-adjusted-cost} to the SSE hardness of total
depth in Corollary~\ref{cor:total-depth-bounded-degree}.
\end{proof}

Earlier work establishes NP-hardness and parameterized lower
bounds for this completion problem~\cite{HogemoEtAl2021,BliznetsEtAl2016}.
The SSE hardness of \emph{chordal} Minimum Fill-In at every fixed factor,
whose target graphs have no induced cycle of length at least four,
is already known~\cite[Theorem~1.2]{APW}. Restricting the
target class to trivially perfect graphs changes the optimum, so
that theorem alone does not prove the statement here.
The incidence construction of Wu, Austrin, Pitassi, and Liu
~\cite[Sections~4.3 and~5]{APW} also gives this hardness on bipartite
inputs. Let $n=|V(G)|$, $N'=|V(G')|=rn+m$, and
$r=nm$, as in Appendix~\ref{app:incidence-proof}. The output is
bipartite and has at most $2rm$ edges. Its YES total depth is at most
$4\varepsilon mN'$, since its treedepth is at most $4\varepsilon m$.
In the NO case, Lemma~\ref{lem:balanced-by-td} gives total depth at
least $(c/2)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,mN'$. The completion identity
subtracts $N'+|E(G')|$, and
\[
 \frac{N'+|E(G')|}{mN'}
 \leq \frac{rn+m+2rm}{m(rn+m)}
 =\frac1m+\frac{2n}{n^2+1}
 \leq \frac4n,
\]
where the last inequality uses $m\geq n/2$, since the host is
regular and $m>0$. For each fixed $\varepsilon$, we may exclude the
finitely many source instances below any fixed order, so assume
$n\geq16/(c\sqrt{\varepsilon})$. The subtraction is then at most
$(c/4)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,mN'$. Therefore the NO completion cost is
at least $(c/4)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,mN'$, whereas the YES cost is at
most $4\varepsilon mN'$. Their ratio is at least
$c/(16\sqrt{\varepsilon})$, which exceeds any fixed constant for a
sufficiently small fixed $\varepsilon$. Hence the completion problem
is SSE-hard to approximate within every fixed constant on bipartite
inputs as well.

\subsection{Deletion and labeling objectives}

\paragraph{Vertex integrity and fragmentation.}
For $c_J(S)$ the largest component order of $J-S$, vertex integrity is
$\operatorname{vi}(J)=\min_S(|S|+c_J(S))$, with $c_J(V(J))=0$.
The elementary inequality
$\td(J)\leq\operatorname{vi}(J)$ follows by putting $S$ on a chain
and its components on separate chains below it. In the layered graph,
deleting all copies of a minimum source vertex cover leaves $q$ separate
cliques on $\alphaI(G)$ vertices. Thus the same YES construction gives
$\operatorname{vi}(S_q(G))\leq q(n-\alphaI(G))+\alphaI(G)$,
while Theorem~\ref{thm:ugc-structural} supplies the NO lower bound.
The parameter choices of Theorem~\ref{thm:ugc-hardness} therefore
also give UGC hardness for vertex integrity below $4/3$.
For SSE hardness, let $J=\mathcal L(G^*)$ be the output in the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sse-hardness}, and take $D$ to be the output vertices
corresponding to crossing host edges. The YES construction supplies
$|D|+c_J(D)\leq4\varepsilon |V(J)|$. In the NO case, the separator
lemma with $\rho=2/3$ gives $b_{2/3}(J)\geq\beta |V(J)|$, where
$b_{2/3}(J)$ is the minimum size of a set whose deletion leaves every
component of order at most $2|V(J)|/3$, and
$\beta=(c/2)\sqrt\varepsilon$. Choose $\varepsilon$ small enough
that $\beta\leq1/12$. For any $S\subseteq V(J)$, if
$c_J(S)>|V(J)|/2$, then $|S|+c_J(S)>|V(J)|/2\geq\beta|V(J)|$.
Otherwise every component of $J-S$ has at most $|V(J)|/2$ vertices,
so $S$ is a $2/3$-balanced separator and
$|S|\geq b_{2/3}(J)\geq\beta|V(J)|$. Thus
$\operatorname{vi}(J)\geq\beta|V(J)|$.
The ratio of the NO lower bound to the YES upper bound grows as
$1/\sqrt\varepsilon$. Thus, for every fixed factor, a sufficiently
small fixed $\varepsilon$ separates the two cases. Hence vertex integrity has no constant-factor
approximation under SSEH.

The \emph{fracture number} is
$\operatorname{fr}(J)=\min_S\max\{|S|,c_J(S)\}$, and
\emph{starwidth} $\operatorname{sw}(J)$ is the minimum width of
a tree decomposition whose underlying tree is a star.
The standard comparisons
$\operatorname{fr}\leq\operatorname{vi}\leq2\operatorname{fr}$ and
$\operatorname{sw}+1\leq\operatorname{vi}\leq2(\operatorname{sw}+1)$
give SSE hardness at every fixed factor for fracture number. For
starwidth, the YES and NO bounds on vertex integrity give
$\operatorname{sw}(J)\leq4\varepsilon N$ and
$\operatorname{sw}(J)\geq\beta N/2-1$, respectively, where
$N=|V(J)|$. Since $\beta/(8\varepsilon)=c/(16\sqrt\varepsilon)$
can be made arbitrarily large, choose $\varepsilon$ for the desired
factor. Replacing every edge of the host $G^*$ by the same fixed
number of parallel copies preserves its normalized partition and
expansion bounds, while multiplying $m=|V(J)|$ and the linear YES
upper bound and NO lower bound by that number. Since $m>0$, a sufficiently large
fixed number of copies absorbs the additive $1$. These comparisons
and an $O(\log\OPT)$ approximation
for vertex integrity are recorded by Gima et
al.~\cite[Section 1.3 and Theorem 5.1]{GimaEtAlIntegrity}.
Earlier NP-hardness includes vertex integrity on connected line
graphs~\cite[Corollary 4.11]{GimaEtAlIntegrity} and starwidth~\cite{VanEe2017}.

\paragraph{General and average customizable labels.}
A customizable hub labeling assigns $L(v)\subseteq V(J)$ so that
every $s$--$t$ path meets $L(s)\cap L(t)$, including the zero-length
path at a vertex~\cite{BlumStorandt2023}.
The labeling is \emph{hierarchical} if there is one vertex order
such that every member of $L(v)$ is ranked at least as high as
$v$. Without this requirement we call it general. Average,
maximum, and total label size mean, respectively,
$\sum_v|L(v)|/|V(J)|$, $\max_v|L(v)|$, and $\sum_v|L(v)|$.
Blum and Storandt prove
$\sum_v|L(v)|/|V(J)|\geq b_{2/3}(J)/4$
\cite[Corollary 10, full version]{BlumStorandt2023}.
Choose a fixed rational $\varepsilon>0$ so that
$\beta=(c/2)\sqrt\varepsilon\leq1/12$ and
$\sqrt\varepsilon<c/(32C)$, where $C\ge1$ is the desired factor.
For the resulting graph $J=\mathcal L(G^*)$, the YES forest provides hierarchical
labels by assigning each vertex its ancestors, including itself.
Take any $s$--$t$ path and choose a vertex $z$ of minimum depth on it.
Every edge of $J$ joins comparable vertices in the forest. Moving from
$z$ along the path in either direction, the next vertex remains below
$z$: if it is an ancestor of the preceding vertex, it cannot lie above
$z$, since that would give it smaller depth. Thus $z$ is a common
ancestor of $s$ and $t$, so these are valid customizable labels. Each
label has size at most $4\varepsilon |V(J)|$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:linegraph-separators}
with $\rho=2/3$ gives NO average at least
$(c/8)\sqrt\varepsilon\,|V(J)|$. Consequently every constant
approximation factor is SSE-hard for average or maximum general
label size and average or maximum hierarchical label size. The same holds for
the average upward vertex search-space size in a customizable
contraction hierarchy (CCH). Here an elimination order adds edges
between the remaining neighbors of each eliminated vertex. Orient
the resulting edges toward the later vertex in that order.
The upward search space of $v$ is the set of vertices reachable
from $v$ along these oriented edges, including $v$ itself.
Total label size is average size times the
graph order and has the same gap. The CCH interpretation follows
from~\cite[Corollary 5, full version]{BlumStorandt2023}.

\subsection{Transfers through established reductions}

The following consequences use existing correspondences. We state
the transformations and their losses to specify which parameter
gaps they preserve.

\paragraph{Automata and restricted cycle rank.}
The \emph{star height} of a regular language is the minimum nesting
depth of Kleene stars among regular expressions describing it.
A star denotes arbitrary finite repetition. A partial deterministic
finite automaton (DFA) has an initial state, accepting states, and
at most one transition from each state for each alphabet letter.
Its language consists of the words read along paths from the
initial state to an accepting state.
It is \emph{bideterministic} if it has one accepting state and
reversing every transition while swapping the initial and accepting
states again gives a DFA. It is \emph{trim} if every state lies
on an initial-to-accepting path. A binary alphabet has two letters.
For a digraph $D$, its \emph{cycle rank} $\operatorname{cr}(D)$
is zero when $D$ is acyclic, is
$1+\min_v\operatorname{cr}(D-v)$ when $D$ is strongly connected
and has a directed cycle, and otherwise is the maximum cycle
rank of its strongly connected components. These components are
the maximal sets whose vertices can reach one another by directed
paths~\cite[Sections~2--3]{Gruber2012}.

Gruber's established cycle-rank/star-height correspondence and
polynomial binary encoding~\cite[Theorems 20 and 23]{Gruber2012}
transfer all three treedepth gaps to ordinary language star height
for a language represented by a binary bideterministic partial DFA.
To preserve the value,
add a universal vertex $a$, adjacent to every vertex of $G$,
replace graph edges by their
two orientations, give every arc its own letter, and take $a$ as
initial and sole final state. Adding the universal vertex gives
$\td(G+a)=\td(G)+1$. The forest correspondence
in~\cite[proof of Theorem~12]{Gruber2012} then gives
$\operatorname{cr}(\overleftrightarrow{G+a})=\td(G)$.
McNaughton's theorem~\cite[Theorem~20]{Gruber2012} and the binary
encoding~\cite[Theorem~23]{Gruber2012} preserve this value.
The trim binary automaton also realizes the gaps as cycle-rank gaps
on strongly connected digraphs of indegree and outdegree at most two.
Gruber already proves NP-hardness under related degree
restrictions~\cite[Corollary 24]{Gruber2012}, and the same maps
transfer earlier treedepth hardness~\cite{BNS}.

\paragraph{Edge ranking.}
An \emph{edge ranking} assigns positive integer labels to edges
so that every path containing two distinct edges of the same
label also contains an edge with a larger label. Its minimum
possible largest label is the edge-ranking number
$\operatorname{er}(B)$.
The standard identity
$\operatorname{er}(B)=\td(\mathcal L(B))$~\cite{BodlaenderRankings}
applied to our explicit SSE hosts yields SSE hardness at every fixed
factor.
It also yields the consequence on simple bipartite hosts. Replace
each loop occurrence by a pendant edge, then subdivide every edge
occurrence once. For an original edge occurrence $e=uv$, subdivision
creates two edges $ux_e$ and $x_ev$, where $x_e$ is a new vertex.
The corresponding two vertices of the new line graph are adjacent.
Contracting this pair for every $e$ recovers the original line graph.
Treedepth is minor-monotone, so this gives a lower bound by the old
line-graph treedepth. Conversely, replacing each vertex of an
elimination forest of the original line graph by a two-vertex chain
gives a forest for the new line graph with at most twice the height.
Thus the transformation loses at most a factor of two. Since the SSE
hardness holds for every fixed factor, apply it first at factor $2C$
to obtain hardness at any desired factor $C$ after this loss.
Edge-ranking hardness itself is already known
from Lam and Yue~\cite{LamYue1998}.

\paragraph{Hypergraph widths and binary queries.}
A hypergraph has a vertex set and a collection of subsets called
hyperedges, and its \emph{rank} is the largest hyperedge size.
A tree decomposition covers each hyperedge by a bag and has the
same connectedness requirement for each vertex as a graph
decomposition. An edge cover of a bag is a set of hyperedges
whose union contains it. These covering edges are also called
\emph{guards}. The \emph{generalized hypertree width}
$\operatorname{ghw}$ minimizes the maximum number of guards in
a bag. The \emph{fractional hypertree width} $\operatorname{fhw}$
instead assigns a separate nonnegative weight to each hyperedge
at each bag, requires the
weights covering each bag vertex to sum to at least one, and
minimizes the maximum total weight at a bag.
For ordinary \emph{hypertree width} $\operatorname{hw}$, use
integral covers and root the decomposition tree. At each node,
any vertex in a chosen guard that also occurs in a descendant
bag must already occur in the current bag. This is the
\emph{special condition}~\cite[Definitions~2.4--2.7]{GottlobEtAlHypertree}.

Marx reduces treewidth to fractional hypertree width
in~\cite[proof of Theorem~5.1]{Marx2010Fractional}. We use adjacent
clone pairs to transfer all three widths on
rank-two instances, with the proof given below. Define $G[K_2]$ on
$V(G)\times\{1,2\}$: distinct vertices $(u,i),(v,j)$ are adjacent
if and only if $u=v$ or $uv\in E(G)$. Thus each source vertex is
replaced by an adjacent pair, and a source edge joins the two pairs
completely. Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{fhw}(G[K_2])=\operatorname{ghw}(G[K_2])
 =\operatorname{hw}(G[K_2])=\operatorname{tw}(G)+1,
\]
where edges of $G[K_2]$ are viewed as size-two hyperedges, with the
standard width definitions of~\cite{GottlobEtAlHypertree}.
For the upper bound, expand every tree-decomposition bag of $G$
to clone pairs and guard it by the pair edges. The guards are
contained in the bag, so the special condition for ordinary
hypertree width holds as well. For the lower bound, consider a
fractional decomposition of width $w$. If $B_t$ is one of its bags
and $\gamma_t(e)$ is the weight on hyperedge $e$, then each vertex
of $B_t$ receives total incident weight at least one. Since every
hyperedge has size two,
$|B_t|\leq\sum_e\gamma_t(e)|e\cap B_t|\leq2\sum_e\gamma_t(e)\leq2w$.
Retain an original vertex in a bag if and only if both of its clones
occur there. Each retained vertex accounts for two bag vertices, so
each retained bag has size at most $2w/2=w$. The bags containing a
retained vertex form a connected nonempty subtree: the two clone
subtrees intersect, since the pair is an edge, and their intersection
is connected. If $uv\in E(G)$, its four clones form a clique, so their
bag subtrees intersect pairwise. Subtrees of a tree have the Helly
property: any finite family that intersects pairwise has a common
node. Hence one bag contains all four clones. The retained bags form
a tree decomposition of $G$ with bag size at most $w$, proving the
reverse inequality.
Thus the NP $r<12/11$, UGC $r<4/3$, and established SSE treewidth
hardness at every fixed factor transfer even to rank-two hypergraphs.
The transfer compares the width value $\operatorname{tw}(G)+1$, which
is the size of a largest bag. Suppose an output graph has order $N$,
YES treewidth at most $aN$, and NO treewidth at least $bN$, where
$b/a>r$. After adding one, the ratio is at least
$(bN+1)/(aN+1)\to b/a>r$. Thus the factor-$r$ gap remains for
sufficiently large $N$.
In the usual query representation, vertices are variables and
each hyperedge supplies one relation atom. Their conjunction is
a conjunctive query, and size-two hyperedges give binary atoms.
Using a distinct relation symbol for each atom therefore gives
the same structural width consequences for these queries.

\subsection{Binary junction-tree total state space}

The width gaps in Sections~\ref{sec:tw-transfer}
and~\ref{subsec:np-treewidth} are linear in the
output order. Exponentiating bag sizes therefore yields exponential
approximation gaps for the total size of dense binary tables.

For an $n$-vertex graph $G$ with $n\ge1$, the established dense-table objective is
$\operatorname{TSS}(G)=\min_{(T,(B_t))}\sum_t2^{|B_t|}$.
The minimum ranges over tree decompositions of $G$.
Each vertex represents a binary variable. The table for bag
$B_t$ has one entry for each of its $2^{|B_t|}$ assignments.
Thus this sum is the total number of table entries in the
corresponding junction tree.
Bodlaender and Fomin identify the equivalent chordal-completion
objective and already prove constant-factor hardness~\cite[Lemma 2,
Section 8, and Theorem 32]{BodlaenderFomin2005}. The elementary bounds
$2^{\operatorname{tw}(G)+1}\leq\operatorname{TSS}(G)\leq n2^{\operatorname{tw}(G)+1}$
follow from a decomposition with at most $n$ bags and optimal width,
obtained from the maximal cliques of an optimal chordal completion,
and from the fact that every decomposition has a bag of size at least
$\operatorname{tw}(G)+1$. They give
NP hardness within $2^{\chi n}$ for every fixed $0<\chi<1/18$,
and UGC hardness for every fixed $0<\chi<1/6$.
For each reduction, subtract the YES treewidth upper bound from the
NO treewidth lower bound. The resulting differences are
\[
 \begin{array}{lll}
 \textup{NP:}&Rm(1/2-2\epsilon)-3m
   =\bigl(1/18-2\epsilon/9-1/(3R)\bigr)n,
   &n=9Rm,\\[4pt]
 \textup{UGC:}&RN(1/6-2\zeta)-N/3
   =\bigl(1/6-2\zeta-1/(3R)\bigr)n,
   &n=RN.
 \end{array}
\]
For NP hardness, choose $\epsilon>0$ so that
$1/18-2\epsilon/9>\chi$, then take $R$ large enough that
$1/(3R)<1/18-2\epsilon/9-\chi$. For UGC, choose $\zeta>0$ so that
$1/6-2\zeta>\chi$, then take $R$ large enough that
$1/(3R)<1/6-2\zeta-\chi$. In either case, let $\delta>0$ be the
remaining margin, so the relevant coefficient exceeds $\chi$ by
$\delta$. Let $w_Y,w_N$ be the displayed YES upper and NO lower
treewidth bounds. The corresponding bag-size bounds are
$b_Y=w_Y+1$ and $b_N=w_N+1$, so
$b_N-b_Y=w_N-w_Y\geq(\chi+\delta)n$. The TSS bounds then imply
\[
 \frac{\operatorname{TSS}(G_N)}{\operatorname{TSS}(G_Y)}
 \geq\frac{2^{b_N}}{n2^{b_Y}}
 \geq\frac{2^{(\chi+\delta)n}}{n}>2^{\chi n}
\]
for all sufficiently large $n$, since $2^{\delta n}>n$ then.
The source instances may be replaced by any fixed number of disjoint
copies without changing their promise fractions. Taking enough copies
therefore ensures that every output order lies in this range.
Bonnet's earlier linear width gap~\cite{BonnetTreewidth} also gives
a fixed positive exponential exponent by the same calculation.

\paragraph{Equivalent formulations.}
In a \emph{vertex ranking}, every path whose distinct endpoints
have the same label has an internal vertex with a larger label.
A \emph{centered coloring} gives every nonempty connected induced
subgraph a color occurring once. The minimum numbers of
labels or colors in these two definitions equal treedepth.
These, together with symbolic elimination height,
minimum clique number of a trivially perfect completion, maximum
hierarchical customizable label size, and maximum CCH vertex
search-space size are established treedepth formulations
\cite{Reidl2014,BodlaenderRankings,BlumStorandt2023}. These formulations
are covered by the same theorem.
