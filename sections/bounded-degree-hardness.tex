\section{Hardness on graphs of bounded degree}
\label{sec:bounded-degree-hardness}

\subsection{The degree-reduction approach}

Let $H$ be one of the dense graphs constructed above, with
$N=|V(H)|$. Its edge set consists of $E_0\subseteq\binom{V(H)}2$,
which we keep during sparsification, and clique and biclique factors
indexed by finite disjoint sets $\mathcal C$ and $\mathcal B$.
Each clique factor $f\in\mathcal C$ has a nonempty support
$U_f\subseteq V(H)$ and contributes
all pairs in $\binom{U_f}{2}$. Each biclique factor has disjoint nonempty sides
$A_f,B_f\subseteq V(H)$, support $U_f=A_f\mathbin{\dot\cup}B_f$, and contributes
all edges $uv$ with $u\in A_f$ and $v\in B_f$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factor-edge-union}
 E(H)=E_0\cup\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal C}\binom{U_f}{2}
       \cup\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal B}\{uv:u\in A_f,\ v\in B_f\}.
\end{equation}
For each factor choose an integer threshold $t_f\ge1$. Two
disjoint vertex sets are \emph{anticomplete} if no edge joins them.
A \emph{$(t,t)$ hole} is a pair of anticomplete sets of $t$ vertices
each. A clique replacement on $U_f$ has no hole at threshold $t_f$
if every two disjoint $t_f$-subsets of $U_f$ have at least one edge
between them. A biclique replacement on
$A_f\mathbin{\dot\cup}B_f$ has no hole at threshold $t_f$ if every
$t_f$-subset of $A_f$ and every $t_f$-subset of $B_f$ have at least
one edge between them.

Different factors may share vertices or edges. A factor specifies
edges to be included, not an induced subgraph, so other factors or
$E_0$ may add further edges on its support.

Replace each factor by a subgraph of its specified edge set on
the same support. Let $J$ be the union of these replacement edges
with $E_0$. Then $J$ is a spanning subgraph of $H$. The argument has
three steps.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \emph{Preserve the YES bound by deleting edges.}
   Every elimination forest or tree decomposition of $H$ is also
   valid for $J$. Thus treedepth, treewidth, and total elimination
   depth do not increase.
 \item \emph{Recover a dense solution from a sparse one.}
   Each replacement satisfies its no-hole condition at threshold
   $t_f$. For clique factors the subsets are disjoint, and for
   biclique factors they lie on opposite sides.
   The completion lemmas show that, after deleting a small set $D$
   of exceptional vertices, an arbitrary forest or decomposition
   of $J$ has every edge of $H-D$ represented by comparable
   endpoints or by vertices in a common bag. For forests,
   restore $D$ as a chain above all existing roots. For tree
   decompositions, add $D$ to every bag. The height or width
   increases by at most $|D|$, and the total depth by at most
   $N|D|$. Thus a sparse solution of value $z$ gives a dense
   solution of height or width at most $z+|D|$, or total depth at
   most $z+N|D|$.
 \item \emph{Keep the total loss below the hardness gap.}
   Choose $t_f$ so that $t_f-1$ is a small fraction of $|U_f|$
   for a clique factor and of $\min\{|A_f|,|B_f|\}$ for a
   biclique factor. If every vertex lies in at most a fixed number
   of factor supports, then $\sum_f |U_f|=O(N)$. Hence
   $|D|\le\varepsilon N$ for any prescribed fixed
   $\varepsilon>0$, by choosing those fractions sufficiently small.
   The resulting losses are at most $\varepsilon N$ for treedepth
   and treewidth, and $\varepsilon N^2$ for total depth. The dense
   gaps have these scales. Lemma~\ref{lem:gap-preservation} therefore
   preserves every factor below the dense hardness threshold when
   $\varepsilon$ is sufficiently small.
\end{enumerate}

For the maximum degree to be bounded, we also need bounded degree
in $E_0$ and a bounded ratio between the two side sizes of every
biclique. This ratio bounds the number of auxiliary vertices mapped
to each prescribed vertex. The expander construction below then
gives the required no-hole condition with a constant degree
contribution from each factor. Since each vertex lies in a bounded number of
factor supports, its total degree is bounded. These constants may
depend on the desired approximation ratio, but not on $N$. For
treewidth, each
side of every biclique must additionally be partitionable into a
bounded number of sets, each contained in a clique-factor support.
The corresponding clique-factor bags anchor the side sets in the
biclique completion lemma.
For the UGC applications, the bounded-degree source promise of
Theorem~\ref{thm:bounded-degree-kr-source} bounds the degree of
the retained source edges $E_0$.

\subsection{Completing sparse factors}

The no-hole conditions above are inherited by deleting vertices. Recall that
forest restriction preserves the ancestor order between surviving
vertices and never increases their depths.

The following lemmas identify a small exceptional set whose deletion
allows a sparse factor to be completed. For a clique, the surviving
vertices must lie on one ancestor path or in one bag. For a biclique,
it is enough that every surviving cross-pair is comparable or appears
together in a bag.

\subsubsection{Clique factors}

\begin{lemma}[Clique completion in an elimination forest]\label{lem:sparse-clique-completion}
Let $J$ be a finite simple graph, let $U\subseteq V(J)$, and let
$t\ge1$ be an integer. Suppose $J[U]$ has no two disjoint
anticomplete sets of size $t$. Every elimination forest $F$ of $J$ has an
ancestor path $P$ of $F$ such that
$|U\setminus V(P)|\leq2(t-1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $|U|<t$, then $|U|\leq t-1\leq2(t-1)$, so the empty path
satisfies the claim. Now assume $|U|\geq t$. Traverse each rooted
tree in depth-first order. Introduce a vertex when first reached,
traverse all its child subtrees, and forget it after those subtrees
have been traversed. Throughout the traversal maintain two subsets
of $U$: $L$ is the set of vertices already forgotten, and $R$ is the
set of vertices not yet introduced. Initially $L=\varnothing$ and
$R=U$. When a vertex of $U$ is introduced, remove it from $R$; when
it is forgotten, add it to $L$. The vertices in $U\setminus(L\cup R)$
have been introduced but not forgotten, so they lie on the current
ancestor path. We traverse the rooted trees one after another, with
this path empty between trees.

At every point, no edge of $J$ joins $L$ to $R$. Indeed, if $x\in L$ and
$y\in R$, then $y$ cannot be an ancestor of $x$, since ancestors are
introduced first. Also, $x$ cannot be an ancestor of $y$, since the
traversal finishes the subtree of $x$ before forgetting $x$. Thus
$x$ and $y$ are incomparable, and the elimination-forest property
rules out the edge $xy$.

Consider the instant immediately after the $t$th vertex of $U$ is
forgotten. Then $|L|=t$. If $|R|\geq t$, then $L$ and $R$ contain
disjoint anticomplete subsets of size $t$, contrary to the
hypothesis. Hence $|R|\leq t-1$. Immediately before this forgetting
event, $|L|=t-1$ and $R$ is unchanged. Let $P$ be the current
ancestor path then. The sets $L$, $R$, and $U\setminus(L\cup R)$
partition $U$, and every vertex in the third set lies on $P$. Hence
\[
 |U\setminus V(P)|\leq |L|+|R|\leq(t-1)+(t-1)=2(t-1).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Clique completion in a tree decomposition]
\label{lem:tw-clique-completion}
Let $J$ be a finite simple graph, let $U\subseteq V(J)$, and let
$t\ge1$ be an integer. Suppose $J[U]$ has no two disjoint
anticomplete sets of size $t$. Every tree decomposition of $J$ has a node $u$ whose bag
$B_u$ satisfies $|U\setminus B_u|\leq3(t-1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $(T,(B_u)_{u\in V(T)})$ be the supplied decomposition.
If $|U|<t$, choose any node $u$ of $T$; then
$|U\setminus B_u|\leq|U|\leq t-1\leq3(t-1)$. Otherwise consider an edge of the
decomposition tree. Its deletion leaves two sides. Call a side
heavy if it contains $T_v$ for at least $t$ vertices $v\in U$.
If both sides were heavy, choose a set $X\subseteq U$ of exactly $t$
vertices with $T_v$ contained in one side, and a set
$Y\subseteq U$ of exactly $t$ vertices with $T_v$ contained in
the other side. The sets are disjoint, since no nonempty $T_v$ can
lie on both sides, so they contain $2t$ vertices in total. For
$x\in X$ and $y\in Y$, we have $T_x\cap T_y=\varnothing$, so no bag
contains both $x$ and $y$. Thus $X$ and $Y$ are anticomplete,
contrary to the hypothesis. Orient the edge toward its
heavy side if one exists, and arbitrarily if neither is heavy.
Following outgoing edges cannot revisit a node, since the underlying
tree has no cycles. The walk therefore ends at a node with no outgoing
edge. Call such a node a \emph{sink}, and let
$u$ be one.

For each component $C$ of $T-u$, at most $t-1$ vertices $v\in U$
have $T_v\subseteq V(C)$. Otherwise, the edge from $u$ into $C$ would be
oriented away from the sink $u$. Let $W=U\setminus B_u$. Every
vertex $v\in W$ has $T_v$ contained in one component of $T-u$;
write $W_C=\{v\in W:T_v\subseteq V(C)\}$. Thus $|W_C|\leq t-1$ for
each component $C$. If $t=1$, this gives $W=\varnothing$. For
$t\geq2$, suppose $|W|\geq3t-2$. Select components one at a time
until the sum of their $|W_C|$ first reaches $t$, and let $W_1$ be
the union of their $W_C$ sets. The last component added contributes at most $t-1$
vertices, so $t\leq|W_1|\leq2t-2$. Let $W_2=W\setminus W_1$;
then $|W_2|\geq(3t-2)-(2t-2)=t$. For $x\in W_1$ and $y\in W_2$,
the sets $T_x,T_y$ lie in different components of $T-u$ and are
therefore disjoint, so no bag contains both $x$ and $y$. Hence $W_1,W_2$ are anticomplete in
$J$, contrary to the hypothesis. Therefore
$|U\setminus B_u|=|W|\leq3t-3=3(t-1)$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Biclique factors}

We use K\"onig's theorem~\cite[Theorem~2.1.1]{Diestel2017}: in a
bipartite graph, the maximum matching size equals the minimum
vertex-cover size.

\begin{lemma}[Biclique completion in an elimination forest]\label{lem:sparse-biclique-completion}
Let $F$ be an elimination forest of a finite simple graph $J$,
let $A,B\subseteq V(J)$ be disjoint, and let $t\ge1$ be an integer.
Suppose the bipartite graph of $J$ between $A$ and $B$ has no
$(t,t)$ hole. There is a set $D\subseteq A\cup B$ such that
$|D|\leq4(t-1)$ and each pair in
$(A\setminus D)\times(B\setminus D)$ is comparable in $F$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $W=A\cup B$ and traverse each rooted tree depth-first: introduce
a vertex when first reached, traverse its child subtrees, and forget
it after they are complete. Maintain the sets $L$ of vertices in $W$
already forgotten and $R$ of vertices in $W$ not yet introduced,
starting with $L=\varnothing$ and $R=W$. When a vertex of $W$ is
introduced, remove it from $R$; when it is forgotten, add it to $L$.
Thus $W\setminus(L\cup R)$ consists of the introduced, unforgotten
vertices on the current ancestor path. Assign a distinct time to
each introduction and forgetting event, traversing different rooted
trees consecutively. Let $t_{\mathrm{in}}(v)$ and
$t_{\mathrm{out}}(v)$ be the introduction and forgetting times of
$v$. The vertices in the subtree rooted at $v$ are visited during
$[t_{\mathrm{in}}(v),t_{\mathrm{out}}(v)]$.
Two vertices are incomparable if and only if these intervals
are disjoint. Define the bipartite graph $K$ by
$E(K)=\{uv:u\in A,\ v\in B,\ u,v\text{ are incomparable in }F\}$.
For each $uv\in E(K)$, take $u\in A$ and $v\in B$, and partition these edges according to
$t_{\mathrm{out}}(u)<t_{\mathrm{in}}(v)$ or $t_{\mathrm{out}}(v)<t_{\mathrm{in}}(u)$.
If $K$ has a matching of size at least $4t-3$, one type contains
at least $2t-1$ matching edges. Consider first the type satisfying
$t_{\mathrm{out}}(u)<t_{\mathrm{in}}(v)$. Write its matching edges
as $u_iv_i$, for $1\le i\le m_0$, where $m_0\ge2t-1$, and order
them so that $t_{\mathrm{out}}(u_1)\le\cdots\le
t_{\mathrm{out}}(u_{m_0})$. For $i\le t$ and
$j\ge m_0-t+1$, we have $i\le j$ and therefore
$t_{\mathrm{out}}(u_i)\le t_{\mathrm{out}}(u_j)<
t_{\mathrm{in}}(v_j)$. Hence the sets
$X=\{u_1,\ldots,u_t\}$ and
$Y=\{v_{m_0-t+1},\ldots,v_{m_0}\}$ each have size $t$, and every
vertex of $X$ is incomparable with every vertex of $Y$. The matching
ensures that the vertices within each set are distinct. Since every
edge of $J$ has comparable endpoints in $F$, the sets are
anticomplete, a contradiction. For the other type, interchange the
roles of $A$ and $B$ and order by the forgetting times of the
vertices in $B$; the same argument gives a contradiction.
It follows that the maximum matching size of $K$ is at most $4t-4$.
K\"onig's theorem supplies a vertex cover $D$ of $K$ of that size.
For $u\in A\setminus D$ and $v\in B\setminus D$, we have
$uv\notin E(K)$ because $D$ covers $K$. By the definition of $K$,
$u,v$ are comparable in $F$.
\end{proof}

For treewidth, we first complete the clique factors and use their
bags to anchor the two sides of each biclique. We use the
path-projection observation from the proof of Bonnet's
Lemma~5~\cite{BonnetTreewidth}: if two subtrees contain opposite
endpoints of a path, they intersect if and only if their
intersections with that path intersect.
The path traces are prefixes and suffixes. The no-hole condition
then bounds a matching of uncovered pairs, and a vertex cover of
those pairs gives the deletion set.

When vertices are deleted from a graph, we restrict its tree
decomposition by removing them from every bag.

\begin{lemma}[Biclique completion between two bags]
\label{lem:tw-biclique-completion}
Let $J$ be a finite simple graph and let $t\ge1$ be an integer.
Fix a tree decomposition $(T,(B_u)_{u\in V(T)})$ of $J$.
Suppose $A,B\subseteq V(J)$ are disjoint, $A\subseteq B_x$ and
$B\subseteq B_y$ for some $x,y\in V(T)$, and the
bipartite graph of $J$ between $A$ and $B$ has no $(t,t)$ hole.
There is a set $D\subseteq A\cup B$ with $|D|\leq2(t-1)$ such that
for every $u\in A\setminus D$ and $v\in B\setminus D$, some
$z\in V(T)$ satisfies $\{u,v\}\subseteq B_z\setminus D$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $x=y$, take $D=\varnothing$. Otherwise let $P$ be the unique
path from $x$ to $y$ in $T$, and list its vertices as
$z_0=x,z_1,\ldots,z_\ell=y$ in their order along the path.
Give $V(P)$ the linear order induced by this list. For $u\in A$,
we have $x\in T_u$, and $T_u$ is connected. Thus
$T_u\cap V(P)=\{z_0,\ldots,z_a\}$ for some $0\leq a\leq\ell$,
the vertex set of a nonempty prefix of $P$. Similarly, for $v\in B$,
$T_v\cap V(P)=\{z_c,\ldots,z_\ell\}$ for some $0\leq c\leq\ell$,
the vertex set of a suffix ending at $y$.
By the observation above, $T_u,T_v$ intersect if and only if these
path intervals intersect.

Define the bipartite graph $K$ by
$E(K)=\{uv:u\in A,\ v\in B,\ T_u\cap T_v=\varnothing\}$.
If it has a matching of size $m_0\ge2t-1$, write its edges
$u_iv_i$, with $u_i\in A$ and $v_i\in B$, and order them so that
the last vertex of $T_{u_i}\cap V(P)$ has nondecreasing index.
The endpoint of each
$u_i$-prefix precedes the start of its matched $v_i$-suffix, since
the two sets are disjoint and the prefix starts at $x$ while the
suffix ends at $y$. Consequently, if $i\le t$ and
$j\ge m_0-t+1$, then $i\le j$ and the prefix for $u_i$ ends before
the suffix for $v_j$ starts. Thus the sets
$X=\{u_1,\ldots,u_t\}$ and
$Y=\{v_{m_0-t+1},\ldots,v_{m_0}\}$ each have size $t$, and
$T_u\cap T_v=\varnothing$ for every $u\in X$, $v\in Y$. The matching ensures
that vertices within each set are distinct. An edge of $J$ requires
its endpoints to occur together in a bag, so $X,Y$ are anticomplete,
contrary to the hypothesis. Therefore the maximum matching size of
$K$ is at most $2t-2$.

K\"onig's theorem gives a vertex cover $D$ of $K$ with
$|D|\le2t-2$. If $u\in A\setminus D$ and $v\in B\setminus D$,
then $uv\notin E(K)$, so $T_u\cap T_v\ne\varnothing$.
Choose $z\in T_u\cap T_v$. Then $\{u,v\}\subseteq B_z\setminus D$,
as required.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Comparison bounds}

\begin{definition}[Graphs and replacement conditions]
\label{def:factor-comparison}
Let $H$ be a finite simple graph whose edges are specified
by~\eqref{eq:factor-edge-union}. For each
$f\in\mathcal C\cup\mathcal B$, fix an integer $t_f\ge1$.
Let $J\subseteq H$ be a spanning subgraph with $E_0\subseteq E(J)$.
Assume the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item For each $f\in\mathcal C$, every two disjoint sets
   $X,Y\subseteq U_f$ with $|X|=|Y|=t_f$ have an edge of $J$
   between them.
 \item For each $f\in\mathcal B$, every $X\subseteq A_f$ and
   $Y\subseteq B_f$ with $|X|=|Y|=t_f$ have an edge of $J$
   between them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Each preceding completion lemma starts with a solution for $J$ and
deletes a small set of vertices so that the same solution also covers
the edges of one completed factor. To cover all edges of $H$, take
the union $D$ of these deletion sets. Further deletions preserve each
completion, so the restricted solution is valid for $H-D$. We can
then restore $D$ above the roots of an elimination forest, or add
$D$ to every bag of a tree decomposition. Thus the number of deleted
vertices controls the increase in height or width. For total depth,
each restored vertex increases the cost by at most $N=|V(H)|$.

For treewidth, we first choose a deletion set $D_{\mathcal C}$
for the clique factors, so each $U_f\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ lies
in a bag. If every biclique side lies in a clique support, these
bags contain $A_f\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ and
$B_f\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$, as required by the biclique
completion lemma. Summing the deletion bounds gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:completion-deletion-bounds}
 \Delta_{\mathrm{td}}=2\sum_{f\in\mathcal C}(t_f-1)+4\sum_{f\in\mathcal B}(t_f-1),
 \qquad
 \Delta_{\mathrm{tw}}=3\sum_{f\in\mathcal C}(t_f-1)+2\sum_{f\in\mathcal B}(t_f-1).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Treedepth comparison]\label{lem:factor-sparsification}
Let $H,J$ and the thresholds $t_f$ satisfy
Definition~\ref{def:factor-comparison}, and let $\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$
be defined by~\eqref{eq:completion-deletion-bounds}.
Every elimination forest $F$ of $J$ can be
completed to an elimination forest $\widehat F$ of $H$ with
$h(\widehat F)\le h(F)+\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$. In particular,
$\td(J)\le\td(H)\le\td(J)+\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix an elimination forest $F$ of $J$. For each $f\in\mathcal C$,
choose the path $P_f$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:sparse-clique-completion}
and put $D_f=U_f\setminus V(P_f)$, so $|D_f|\le2(t_f-1)$.
For each $f\in\mathcal B$, let $D_f\subseteq A_f\cup B_f$ be
the set from Lemma~\ref{lem:sparse-biclique-completion}, with
$|D_f|\le4(t_f-1)$. Set
$D=\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal C\cup\mathcal B}D_f$.
Thus $|D|\le\sum_f|D_f|\le\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$.

Let $F'$ be the restriction of $F$ to $V(H)\setminus D$.
For $f\in\mathcal C$, the set
$U_f\setminus D\subseteq U_f\setminus D_f$ lies on $P_f$,
so every pair in $\binom{U_f\setminus D}{2}$ is comparable in $F'$.
For $f\in\mathcal B$, every pair in
$(A_f\setminus D)\times(B_f\setminus D)$ is comparable in $F'$ by the
choice of $D_f$. Finally, if
$uv\in E_0\cap\binom{V(H)\setminus D}{2}$, then $uv\in E(J)$,
so $u,v$ are comparable in $F$ and remain comparable in $F'$.
By~\eqref{eq:factor-edge-union}, these three cases cover every
edge of $H-D$. Thus $F'$ is an elimination forest of $H-D$ with
$h(F')\le h(F)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:restore-vertices},
restoring $D$ gives an elimination forest $\widehat F$ of $H$ with
$h(\widehat F)\le h(F)+|D|\le h(F)+\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$.
Choosing $F$ of minimum height proves
$\td(H)\le\td(J)+\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$. The other comparison follows from
$J\subseteq H$.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Total-depth comparison]
\label{lem:total-depth-comparison}
Let $H,J$ and the thresholds $t_f$ satisfy
Definition~\ref{def:factor-comparison}. Put $N=|V(H)|$, and let
$\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$ be defined by~\eqref{eq:completion-deletion-bounds}.
Every elimination
forest $F$ of $J$ can be completed to an elimination forest
$\widehat F$ of $H$ with
$\totd(\widehat F)\le\totd(F)+N\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$.
In particular, $\totd(J)\le \totd(H)\le \totd(J)+N\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix any elimination forest $F$ of $J$. Use the exceptional set
$D$ from the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:factor-sparsification} for
this forest, so $|D|\le\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$. That proof shows that the
restriction $F'$ of $F$ to $V(H)\setminus D$ is an elimination
forest of $H-D$.

For each $v\in V(H)\setminus D$, restriction gives
$\operatorname{depth}_{F'}(v)\le\operatorname{depth}_F(v)$.
Summing over $V(H)\setminus D$ gives $\totd(F')\le\totd(F)$. Restore
the vertices of $D$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:restore-vertices}, placing
them one at a time above all existing roots. Each restoration
increases the depths of the existing vertices by one and gives
the new root depth one. Its total cost is therefore the number of
vertices then present, at most $N$. After $|D|$ restorations, the
resulting elimination forest $\widehat F$ satisfies
$\totd(\widehat F)\le\totd(F)+N|D|\le\totd(F)+N\Delta_{\mathrm{td}}$.
Choosing $F$ of minimum total depth proves the upper
comparison. The lower comparison follows from $J\subseteq H$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Treewidth comparison]
\label{lem:tw-factor-sparsification}
Let $H,J$ and the thresholds $t_f$ satisfy
Definition~\ref{def:factor-comparison}, and let $\Delta_{\mathrm{tw}}$
be defined by~\eqref{eq:completion-deletion-bounds}. Suppose that for
every $f\in\mathcal B$ there are $g,h\in\mathcal C$ with
$A_f\subseteq U_g$ and $B_f\subseteq U_h$.
Every tree
decomposition of $J$ can be completed to one of $H$ whose width
increases by at most $\Delta_{\mathrm{tw}}$. In particular,
$\operatorname{tw}(J)\le\operatorname{tw}(H)
 \le\operatorname{tw}(J)+\Delta_{\mathrm{tw}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix a tree decomposition $(T,(B_u)_{u\in V(T)})$ of $J$.
For each $f\in\mathcal C$, choose $u_f$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:tw-clique-completion} and put
$D_f=U_f\setminus B_{u_f}$, so $|D_f|\le3(t_f-1)$.
Set $D_{\mathcal C}=\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal C}D_f$ and restrict
the bags to $B_u\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$.
Then $U_f\setminus D_{\mathcal C}\subseteq
B_{u_f}\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ for each $f\in\mathcal C$.

For $f\in\mathcal B$, choose $g,h\in\mathcal C$ with
$A_f\subseteq U_g$ and $B_f\subseteq U_h$.
The restricted sides $A_f\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ and
$B_f\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ lie in
$B_{u_g}\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ and
$B_{u_h}\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$, respectively. They still
satisfy the no-hole condition at threshold $t_f$, since any
$t_f$-subsets of them were also subsets of the original sides.
Lemma~\ref{lem:tw-biclique-completion} therefore gives
$D_f\subseteq(A_f\cup B_f)\setminus D_{\mathcal C}$ with
$|D_f|\le2(t_f-1)$.
Put $D=D_{\mathcal C}\cup\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal B}D_f$.
Then $|D|\le\sum_f|D_f|\le\Delta_{\mathrm{tw}}$.

For $f\in\mathcal C$, we have
$U_f\setminus D\subseteq B_{u_f}\setminus D$, so this bag covers
$\binom{U_f\setminus D}{2}$.
For $f\in\mathcal B$ and $x\in A_f\setminus D$,
$y\in B_f\setminus D$, the choice of $D_f$ gives a node $u\in V(T)$
with $\{x,y\}\subseteq B_u\setminus D$.
If $xy\in E_0\cap\binom{V(H)\setminus D}{2}$,
an original bag covering $xy\in E(J)$ still contains both endpoints
after restriction. Equation~\eqref{eq:factor-edge-union} now shows
that $(T,(B_u\setminus D)_{u\in V(T)})$ covers every edge of $H-D$.
Each vertex outside $D$ occurs at the same nodes as before, so its
bag occurrences remain nonempty and connected.

Define $\widehat B_u=(B_u\setminus D)\cup D$ for every $u\in V(T)$.
Every vertex of $D$ now occurs in every bag. The edges of $H-D$
remain covered. If $xy\in E(H)$ with $x,y\in D$, every bag contains
both endpoints. If $x\in D$ and $y\notin D$, any bag containing
$y$ also contains $x$.
Thus $(T,(\widehat B_u)_{u\in V(T)})$ is a decomposition of $H$.
Since $|\widehat B_u|\le|B_u|+|D|$ for every $u$, its width
increases by at most $|D|\le\Delta_{\mathrm{tw}}$.
Choosing a minimum-width decomposition proves the upper comparison.
The lower comparison follows from $J\subseteq H$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Sparse replacements at arbitrary sizes}

The dense reductions use a clique to force its vertices onto one
ancestor path in an elimination forest or into one bag of a tree decomposition.
The completion lemmas recover this behavior, after deleting a few
vertices, when a sparse replacement has no two large anticomplete sets.
For bicliques, each surviving pair on opposite sides is
comparable in the forest or occurs together in some bag.
More specifically, a clique replacement need only join every two
disjoint sets of $t_f$ vertices, and a biclique replacement need
only join every pair of $t_f$-sets on opposite sides. Each factor
then contributes at most a constant multiple of $t_f-1$ exceptional
vertices. Our remaining task is to satisfy these conditions with
bounded degree, while bounding $t_f-1$ by a small fixed fraction of
the factor size. This makes the completion loss small enough to
preserve the hardness gap.

The idea is to distribute a few edges at each vertex so that two
large sets cannot avoid each other. Expanders let us do this. We
first use the expander mixing lemma to turn an eigenvalue bound
into an edge between any two sufficiently large sets. We then join
endpoints of fixed-length walks to lower the required set size.
The degree increases, but remains bounded independently of the
number of vertices. Finally, we map these graphs onto the prescribed
clique supports and biclique sides. Lemma~\ref{lem:factor-expanders}
collects the degree and no-hole bounds needed for the comparison
lemmas.

\subsubsection{Edge expansion and adjacency matrices}

We begin with edge expansion, which requires a set to send many
edges to its complement. For our replacements, we must also force
an edge between two prescribed large sets, even when they do not
cover the graph. An eigenvalue bound will control these edge counts
through the mixing lemma.
The definitions and standard spectral facts below follow
\cite[Section~2]{HLW2006}.

Let $\mathcal E$ be a finite undirected multigraph on $M\ge1$
vertices, allowing loops and parallel edges. A \emph{step} traverses
one edge occurrence in a specified direction. A nonloop edge gives
one step in each direction, and a loop gives one step that stays at
its vertex. Parallel edge occurrences give distinct steps. In this
subsection, $d$-regular means that every vertex has $d$ possible
outgoing steps, counting loop steps once.

For vertex sets $S,T\subseteq V(\mathcal E)$, let
$e_{\mathcal E}(S,T)$ be the number of steps that start in $S$ and
end in $T$, counted with multiplicity. The sets may overlap. If
$S=T$, each nonloop internal edge contributes two steps and each loop
contributes one. If $S,T$ are disjoint, each edge between them
contributes one step from $S$ to $T$.
In particular,
$|\cut_{\mathcal E}(S)|=e_{\mathcal E}(S,V(\mathcal E)\setminus S)$.

\begin{definition}[Edge expanders]
For an integer $d\ge1$ and a real $c>0$, a $d$-regular graph $\mathcal E$ is a
\emph{$(d,c)$-edge expander} if
\[
 |\cut_{\mathcal E}(S)|\ge c|S|
 \quad\text{whenever }0<|S|\le M/2.
\]
A sequence of graphs whose orders tend to infinity is a
\emph{bounded-degree expander family} if the same constants $d,c$
work for every graph in the sequence.
\end{definition}

The boundary condition says that separating $S$ from its complement
requires deleting at least $c|S|$ edge occurrences. Bounded degree
keeps the total number of edges linear in the number of vertices.
To bound the edges between two specified sets, we express their
number using the adjacency matrix and then bound its eigenvalues.

Now assume that $\mathcal E$ is $d$-regular. Its \emph{adjacency
matrix} $A_{\mathcal E}$ has rows and columns
indexed by $V(\mathcal E)$, with $(A_{\mathcal E})_{uv}$ equal to the
number of steps from $u$ to $v$. It is symmetric, meaning
$(A_{\mathcal E})_{uv}=(A_{\mathcal E})_{vu}$, because every step
between distinct vertices has a reverse step. Each row sum is $d$.
A real vector $x=(x_v)_{v\in V(\mathcal E)}$ assigns a real number to
each vertex, and matrix multiplication gives
$(A_{\mathcal E}x)_u=\sum_v(A_{\mathcal E})_{uv}x_v$.
Thus this coordinate is the sum of the values at the endpoints of
the steps leaving $u$.

Write $\mathbf1$ for the all-ones vector. For a set $S$, its
\emph{indicator vector} $\mathbf1_S$ has coordinate one on $S$ and
zero elsewhere. A superscript $\mathsf T$ denotes transpose, so
$x^{\mathsf T}y=\sum_vx_vy_v$. The Euclidean length of $x$ is
$\|x\|_2=(\sum_vx_v^2)^{1/2}$. Two vectors are
\emph{orthogonal} if $x^{\mathsf T}y=0$. In particular,
$x$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf1$ if and only if $\sum_vx_v=0$.
The step count can now be written as
$e_{\mathcal E}(S,T)=\mathbf1_S^{\mathsf T}A_{\mathcal E}\mathbf1_T$.

A nonzero vector $x$ is an \emph{eigenvector} of $A_{\mathcal E}$
with \emph{eigenvalue} $\mu$ if $A_{\mathcal E}x=\mu x$.
Since each row sum is $d$, we have
$A_{\mathcal E}\mathbf1=d\mathbf1$. The spectral theorem for real
symmetric matrices states that there is a basis of eigenvectors
that have length one and are pairwise orthogonal, and every vector
is a unique linear combination of these basis vectors. We may take the
first basis vector to be $\mathbf1/\sqrt M$, with eigenvalue $d$.
All remaining basis vectors then have coordinate sum zero.
Moreover, multiplication by $A_{\mathcal E}$ preserves zero sums:
\[
 \mathbf1^{\mathsf T}A_{\mathcal E}x
 =(A_{\mathcal E}\mathbf1)^{\mathsf T}x
 =d\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}x=0
 \quad\text{if }\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}x=0.
\]

Subtracting the average coordinate from every coordinate of a
vector produces a zero-sum vector. Thus zero-sum vectors describe
deviations from a constant assignment. The eigenvalue bound below
controls these deviations and will give the error bound in the
mixing lemma.

\begin{definition}[Spectral expanders]
The graph $\mathcal E$ has \emph{nonconstant eigenvalue bound}
$\lambda\ge0$ if all eigenvalues belonging to the zero-sum
eigenvectors have absolute value at most $\lambda$.
We call it a \emph{spectral expander} when $\lambda<d$.
A sequence of such graphs with orders tending to infinity is a
\emph{bounded-degree spectral expander family} if $d$ is fixed and
$\lambda/d$ has a common upper bound below one, independent of $M$.
\end{definition}

The word nonconstant excludes the chosen all-ones eigenvector,
not any further eigenvectors with the same eigenvalue $d$.
The absolute-value bound controls both positive and negative
eigenvalues, which will be needed when we take walk powers.
The standard spectral lower bound on edge expansion
\cite[Theorem~2.4]{HLW2006} states that a $d$-regular graph with
nonconstant eigenvalue bound $\lambda<d$ satisfies
$|\cut_{\mathcal E}(S)|\ge(d-\lambda)|S|/2$ whenever
$0<|S|\le M/2$. Hence such a spectral family is an expander family
under the preceding definition.

\subsubsection{The mixing lemma and walk powers}

Our goal is to show that the edge count between two large sets is
positive. If the $d|S|$ steps leaving $S$ had endpoints uniformly
distributed over the $M$ vertices, then $d|S||T|/M$ would end in $T$.
The expander mixing lemma bounds the difference between this
quantity and the actual step count. We use its standard form,
including when $S,T$ overlap.

\begin{lemma}[Expander mixing lemma {\cite[Lemma~2.5]{HLW2006}}]
\label{lem:expander-mixing}
Let $\mathcal E$ be a $d$-regular graph on $M$ vertices with
nonconstant eigenvalue bound $\lambda$. For all
$S,T\subseteq V(\mathcal E)$,
\[
 \left|e_{\mathcal E}(S,T)-\frac{d|S||T|}{M}\right|
 \le\lambda\sqrt{|S||T|}.
\]
\end{lemma}

To guarantee a step between $S$ and $T$, it is enough that the
main term exceed the error term. For nonempty $S,T$, the lemma gives
\[
 e_{\mathcal E}(S,T)
 \ge\frac{d|S||T|}{M}-\lambda\sqrt{|S||T|}
 =d\sqrt{|S||T|}\left(\frac{\sqrt{|S||T|}}{M}-\frac\lambda d\right).
\]
Thus $e_{\mathcal E}(S,T)>0$ if
$\sqrt{|S||T|}/M>\lambda/d$. In particular, sets of at least $t$
vertices are joined whenever $t>(\lambda/d)M$. Thus $\lambda/d$
determines the fraction of vertices above which holes are
impossible. A smaller ratio gives a smaller threshold and hence a
smaller completion loss.

To reduce this ratio, we treat each fixed-length walk as a new
step between its endpoints. A walk can reach beyond the immediate
neighbors of its starting vertex, giving more possible endpoints
in a second set. The calculations below show that this operation
reduces the eigenvalue-to-degree ratio while keeping the degree
bounded for any fixed walk length.

For an integer $k\ge0$, a \emph{walk of length $k$} consists of vertices
$x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_k$ and a chosen step from $x_{i-1}$ to $x_i$
for each $1\le i\le k$. Vertices and edges may be repeated.
Different choices among parallel steps give different walks,
even if their vertex sequences agree.
Reversing the order and directions of its steps gives
a walk of the same length in the opposite direction.

For an integer $k\ge1$, the \emph{$k$th walk power} of
$\mathcal E$ has the same vertex set and one step from $u$ to $v$
for each length-$k$ walk from $u$ to $v$ in $\mathcal E$.
Its adjacency matrix is $A_{\mathcal E}^k$. To see this, the
$(u,v)$ entry of $A_{\mathcal E}$ counts length-one walks, and
\[
 (A_{\mathcal E}^{k+1})_{uv}
 =\sum_z(A_{\mathcal E}^k)_{uz}(A_{\mathcal E})_{zv}
\]
counts a length-$k$ walk from $u$ to $z$, followed by a step from
$z$ to $v$. Summing over the penultimate vertex $z$ counts every
length-$(k+1)$ walk once. Reversing walks shows that the walk power
is undirected.

There are $d$ choices at each of the $k$ steps, so each vertex
starts $d^k$ walks and the walk power is $d^k$-regular.
If $A_{\mathcal E}x=\mu x$, then
$A_{\mathcal E}^2x=A_{\mathcal E}(\mu x)=\mu A_{\mathcal E}x=\mu^2x$,
and induction gives $A_{\mathcal E}^kx=\mu^kx$.
The orthonormal eigenbasis of $A_{\mathcal E}$ remains an eigenbasis
of $A_{\mathcal E}^k$. On each zero-sum basis vector, the new
eigenvalue satisfies $|\mu^k|=|\mu|^k\le\lambda^k$.
Consequently the walk power has nonconstant eigenvalue bound $\lambda^k$,
and the ratio of this bound to its degree is
$\lambda^k/d^k=(\lambda/d)^k$.
When $\lambda/d<1$, a fixed sufficiently large $k$ makes this ratio
smaller than any prescribed positive constant. The new degree
$d^k$ still does not depend on the number of vertices.

We use the following explicit starting family.

\begin{theorem}[Margulis--Gabber--Galil expanders
 {\cite[Construction~8.1 and Theorem~8.2]{HLW2006}}]
\label{thm:auxiliary-expander-family}
For every integer $\nu\ge1$, there is a deterministically
constructible eight-regular undirected multigraph on $\nu^2$
vertices whose nonconstant eigenvalues have absolute value
at most $5\sqrt2$. Its construction takes polynomial time in $\nu$.
\end{theorem}

The eigenvalue-to-degree ratio is at most $5\sqrt2/8<1$. Thus, for
any fixed positive threshold fraction, a sufficiently large fixed
walk power gives the required no-hole bound. These graphs are
available on $\nu^2$ vertices. Our remaining task is to obtain the
same type of guarantee on arbitrary prescribed vertex sets and,
for biclique factors, on two prescribed sides with all edges
between them. This is a standard adaptation by grouping auxiliary
vertices. We state the resulting lemma below and give the mapping
definitions and proof in Appendix~\ref{app:factor-expanders}.

\begin{lemma}[Mapping to prescribed vertex sets]
\label{lem:factor-expanders}
For every fixed $\zeta>0$ and $\kappa\ge1$, there is a constant
$d_{\zeta,\kappa}$ such that the following connected simple graphs,
each of maximum degree at most $d_{\zeta,\kappa}$, can be constructed
deterministically in polynomial time.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item For any specified nonempty finite set $U$, a graph on $U$
 in which every two disjoint sets $X,Y\subseteq U$ with
 $|X|,|Y|\ge\lceil\zeta|U|\rceil$ have an edge between them.
 \item For any specified disjoint nonempty finite sets $A,B$ with
 $\max\{|A|,|B|\}/\min\{|A|,|B|\}\le\kappa$, a bipartite graph
 on $A\mathbin{\dot\cup}B$ with parts $A,B$ in which every two sets
 $X\subseteq A$, $Y\subseteq B$ with
 $|X|,|Y|\ge\lceil\zeta(|A|+|B|)\rceil$ have an edge between them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
